Will Writing

Your wishes, written down properly, so they're carried out.

We make sure all your wishes are correctly documented, so that when the time comes they can be carried out.

Book a consultation Who inherits without a Will?
Photo: Couple at a kitchen table with Sevvy, going through documents

Over 60% of people don't have a Will. Of those who do, around 20% are out of date, invalid or not fit for purpose.

Without a Will, the law decides who inherits, and there's no direct provision for partners or stepchildren.

What goes into your Will

The key decisions

Executors

The people you trust to carry out your wishes.

Guardians

Who will look after your children if they are under 18.

Beneficiaries

Who inherits from you, through specific gifts and the residue.

Funeral wishes

Burial or cremation, organ donation and the ceremony.

Exclusions

Leaving someone out, and explaining why.

Complex Wills

Inheritance Tax, blended families, business and foreign property.

Is a Will enough?

For most modern families, a Will alone isn't enough

A Will only covers what happens after you die. What you own now, you may not own later. And if you lose mental capacity, a Will can't help.

FAQs

Questions about Wills

Who should I choose as my executors?

Executors carry out the wishes in your Will, so choose someone trustworthy and responsible. Commonly that's a spouse or partner, adult children, a family member or a close friend. It's a good idea to appoint more than one, in case one is unable or unwilling to act, and you can also appoint “reserve executors” as back-ups.

A professional executor can be appointed if you'd prefer an expert to handle complex matters, which can often be more efficient, reliable and cost-effective.

Do I need to appoint guardians?

If a child loses their parents before 18, the local authority becomes responsible and must do what it considers to be in the child's best interests. That may be placing them with family, but could include foster care or even adoption. In your Will you can name people who share your values and whom you trust to raise your children.

Things to consider: do your children and the guardians get on? Do the guardians' own children get on with yours? Can they afford it, and should you make financial provision? Do they have the time and energy? Is their house large enough? How close do they live to you and your children's schools?

What are legacies and the residue?

Legacies are optional specific gifts to individuals, charities or organisations, such as money, property, jewellery or artwork. With many items, a separate list is often kept with the Will and can be updated without drafting a new one.

The residue is what's left after funeral expenses, debts, Inheritance Tax and legacies. You choose who receives it and in what percentages, and what should happen if a beneficiary dies before you. A child can't inherit below 18, and you may choose an older age, often 21 or 25.

Can I leave someone out of my Will?

Yes. If you want to exclude someone, it's important to mention this in your Will, normally with a non-vindictive, handwritten letter kept with it explaining why. This can help avoid disputes. Be aware that certain family members and dependants can still make a claim if they believe they haven't been reasonably provided for.

Are my funeral wishes binding?

No. Your Will can include whether you prefer burial or cremation, whether you're an organ donor and requests for the ceremony. These aren't legally binding but guide your loved ones and executors. Quite often a Will isn't read until after a funeral, so it's more important that your family know your wishes.

Who can witness my Will?

Two independent witnesses are required. Both must be present together with you, and all three of you must see each other sign. Witnesses cannot be anyone named as a beneficiary, or their spouses or partners. The witnessing is done on the last page, so they won't see the content; it's common to ask neighbours, close friends or work colleagues.

When should I review my Will?

A Will is drafted for your circumstances at the time, and although every attempt is made to “future-proof” it, family circumstances and legislation continually change. Review it every few years, and on any major life event affecting you or those named in it: births and deaths; cohabitation, marriage, civil partnership, separation or divorce; moving home; buying or selling property; receiving a windfall or inheritance; or starting or closing a business.

What is a complex Will?

One involving Inheritance Tax advice, complex family structures, business interests, land ownership or foreign property. If relevant, these are discussed in full during your appointment. We'd need details such as company names and numbers, other directors or partners, shareholdings and where in the world assets are located.

Isn't a Will enough?

A Will simply documents who gets whatever you have after you've gone. But what you have now, you may not have when the time comes. Tens of thousands of homes are sold each year to pay for care fees, which is why people often use Trusts. A Will also can't help if you lose mental capacity; for that you need Lasting Powers of Attorney.
