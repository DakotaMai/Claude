Two simple ways to think about a Trust

{{icon:shield}}

A legal fence

If you wanted to keep people off your land, you'd build a fence. A Trust is a legal fence around your home, savings or a child's inheritance that says “hands off”.

{{icon:clock}}

A delayed gift

A gift handed over later, when the time is right. For example, when a child comes of age, or once a partner no longer needs to live in the home.

Why people use Trusts

What a Trust can protect against

Once a gift has been made in a Will, it belongs to the person who receives it, with all the risks that come with that. A Trust keeps control.

Types of Trust

The Trusts we use most often

Protective Property Trust (PPT)

Standard advice for any couple who own their home together, and very cost-effective.

Most couples own their home as “joint tenants”, so when one dies the survivor automatically owns all of it, whatever the Will says. That whole home is then exposed to care fees, debt, remarriage (“sideways disinheritance”) or a stepparent changing their Will.

Instead, the home is owned as “tenants in common”, with each person owning a share. In their Will, each person leaves their share into Trust. The survivor can stay in the home for life, but the share in Trust is guaranteed to reach your chosen beneficiaries.

What it protects

  • If the survivor needs care, only their own share can be assessed
  • A new spouse can only inherit the survivor's share
  • Beneficiaries' divorce or debts can't touch it until they inherit
  • Options to allow downsizing or to release funds if needed

Worth knowing: It only takes effect on first death, only protects the property, and needs two or more owners. For fuller protection, look at a Family Protection Trust.

Family Protection Trust (FPT)

A Trust in a Will can't do anything until someone dies. An FPT is a separate document set up now, so the protection starts straight away. It works for sole owners too, and can hold investments as well as property (with separate financial advice).

You (the “Settlor”) place an asset with trusted people (the “Trustees”) to look after for the people who'll eventually benefit (the “Beneficiaries”). You can be a Trustee and a Beneficiary yourself.

When you set up an FPT, a Protective Property Trust is also included in your Will as standard.

Five main things it can help with

  • Probate: no probate on a mortgage-free home held in Trust, so beneficiaries get it quickly
  • Challenges: can't be contested once it's been in place for six years
  • Divorce: courts can't take account of what's locked in Trust
  • Debt: an inheritance held in Trust can't be taken by creditors
  • Inheritance Tax: funds can pass down generations without being added to a beneficiary's estate

Important: An FPT is for protecting the next generation. It isn't a way to avoid care fees. Assets moved deliberately to avoid care fees or creditors (“deliberate deprivation”) can be clawed back, so timing and genuine reasons matter, and it's never a guarantee.

Life Interest & Right to Occupy Trusts

Ideal for second marriages, new partners and blended families. Instead of leaving the home outright, you let someone live there, rent-free and as if it were their own, while it's guaranteed to pass to your chosen people afterwards.

Example: a homeowner with children has a new partner move in. Leave the house to the children and the partner could be made homeless and challenge the Will. Leave it to the partner and it could end up with their family instead of your children. A Life Interest solves both problems.

A Life Interest lasts for life. A Right to Occupy ends on events you choose, such as remarriage, moving in with someone new, going into long-term care, or not maintaining the property.

Things to consider

  • The home's value may count towards the occupant's estate for Inheritance Tax
  • If the occupant is much younger, your beneficiaries may wait many years
  • Can also be used for cash and investments, with the income going to the beneficiary
  • Trustees can be given money to pay bills, and the right to inspect the property

Discretionary Trust

Control “from beyond the grave”. Instead of fixed shares, everything goes into one “pot” and your Trustees decide who receives what, and when, guided by a confidential Letter of Wishes from you.

Because no one is entitled to a set amount, a beneficiary's potential share can't be counted against them. That protects them from divorce, a partner who's after their money, creditors, or losing means-tested benefits (the savings cap is currently £16,000).

It's also private. A Will becomes a public record, but your Letter of Wishes doesn't.

Particularly useful when a beneficiary…

  • Is going through a divorce or difficult relationship
  • Has debts or financial difficulties
  • Isn't good with money: gambling, addiction or a learning difficulty
  • Has a disability and relies on means-tested benefits
  • Might marry young and inherit before you'd want them to

Some countries, particularly in Europe, don't recognise Trusts. If a beneficiary lives abroad, other options may be needed.
