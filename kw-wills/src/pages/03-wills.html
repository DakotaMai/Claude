Will Writing

Your wishes, written down properly, so they're carried out.

A Will decides who sorts things out, who looks after your children and who inherits what. Without one, the law decides for you. Sevvy will make sure every detail is right, and explain it all in plain English.

{{icon:calendar}} Book a free consultation {{icon:tree}} Who inherits without a Will?

Over 60% of people don't have a Will, and around 1 in 5 Wills are out of date, invalid or not fit for purpose.

If you die without a valid Will you die “intestate”, and a pecking order laid down in law decides who inherits. There is no provision for unmarried partners or stepchildren.

What goes into your Will

The key decisions, explained

At your appointment we'll talk through each of these together. Here's a head start.

{{icon:users}}

Executors

The people you trust to carry out your wishes. Usually a spouse or partner, adult children, family or close friends. It's wise to name more than one, plus reserves. A professional executor can be appointed if your affairs are complex.

{{icon:heart}}

Guardians

Who will raise your children if they lose both parents before 18? Without a named guardian, the local authority decides, and that could even mean foster care. You can choose people who share your values.

{{icon:tree}}

Beneficiaries

Who inherits: specific gifts (“legacies”) of money, property or treasured items, and the “residue”, which is everything left once debts and costs are paid. You can also choose an older age to inherit, such as 21 or 25.

{{icon:will}}

Funeral wishes

Burial or cremation, organ donation and any requests for the ceremony. They aren't legally binding, but they guide your loved ones. Tell your family too, as Wills are often read after the funeral.

{{icon:shield}}

Exclusions

If you want to leave someone out, it's important to say so, usually with a calm, handwritten letter kept with your Will explaining why. That helps reduce the risk of a dispute later.

{{icon:briefcase}}

Complex Wills

Inheritance tax planning, blended families, business interests, land and property abroad. We cover them all, and fully, during your appointment.

Is a Will enough?

For most modern families, a Will alone isn't enough

A Will simply records who gets what you have when you die. The trouble is that what you have now, you may not have then. Tens of thousands of homes are sold every year to pay for care fees, and inheritances can be lost to divorce, debt or remarriage.

That's why many people add a Trust to their Will. And because a Will only helps after you die, Lasting Powers of Attorney protect you if you lose the ability to manage your own affairs while you're still alive.

Find out about Trusts

Consider a Trust if you…

  • Own your home, especially jointly
  • Have stepchildren or children from a previous relationship
  • Are in a second marriage or civil partnership
  • Have a vulnerable family member, through disability or money problems
  • Intend to leave a family member out of your Will
Keeping it up to date

When should you review your Will?

Official advice is to review your Will every few years, and straight away after big life events, whether they affect you or the people named in it.

  • Births and deaths
  • Marriage or civil partnership
  • Separation or divorce
  • Moving in together
  • Moving home
  • Buying or selling property
  • Receiving an inheritance or windfall
  • Starting or closing a business

Getting it right

Signing your Will properly

Who can witness my Will?

You need two independent adult witnesses, present together with you, and all three of you must see each other sign. A witness cannot be a beneficiary, or the spouse or partner of one. Neighbours, friends and work colleagues are common choices. They sign on the last page, so they won't see what your Will says.

What happens to the original?
